# Radical Addition to 2‑Allenylaryl Isocyanides for the Preparation of 2,3-Difunctionalized Quinolines

Till Drennhaus, Jens Birschel, Constantin G. Daniliuc, Dirk Leifert,\* and Armido Studer\*

ABSTRACT: 2-Allenylaryl isocyanides are presented as a novel platform to construct quinolines carrying various substituents in positions 2 and 3 via radical addition cyclization cascades. Carbon-, sulfur-, and tin-centered radicals add to the isonitrile functionality to generate the corresponding imidoyl radicals, which undergo 6- exo-cyclization onto the ortho-allenyl moiety. The newly generated benzylic radicals are either reduced or cyanated. In the latter case, enantioselective trapping is achieved.

This enables isocyanides to form�unlike other radical acceptors such as alkenes, alkynes, nitriles, etc.�1,1-adducts on the terminal carbon, thereby acting as C1-synthons.<sup>2</sup> The eficiency of such processes renders the isonitrile functionality a highly versatile radical acceptor to realize radical addition cyclization cascades, as initial radical addition proceeds almost exclusively on the isocyanide functionality in the presence of various other typical radical acceptors.<sup>2</sup> Despite being known for their great performance in radical reactions since the $1 9 6 0 s , ^ { 5 }$ it was not until the 1990s that Curran introduced this functional group in radical cascade reactions,<sup>6</sup> laying the foundation for related strategies for the synthesis of various N-heterocyclic natural products.<sup>7</sup> Over the last three decades, aryl isocyanides have been intensively used in radical chemistry, enabling access to valuable products.<sup>3,4,8,9</sup>

In particular, ortho-acceptor-substituted aryl isocyanides 1 stand out by their remarkable eficiency for the construction of diverse N-heteroarenes.<sup>9</sup> The imidoyl radicals 2 formed after initial radical addition to the isocyanide 1 are prone to cyclize onto pending ortho-radical acceptors. Mainly, the nature of the ortho-acceptor moiety directs the reaction outcome, enabling access to a large series of important N-heterocyclic products (Scheme 1A). Quinolines represent an important compound class, as various members are associated with useful biological activities, which renders the synthesis of these heterocycles a desired target (Scheme 1B). While most classical strategies exploit inter- or intramolecular cyclization of nitrogen-containing fragments with fitting carbon-based counterparts (e.g., classic Skraup or Doebner−Miller synthesis), extensive efort has been recently spent to expand the scope of accessible quinolines via mild catalytic methods.<sup>27</sup> Along these lines, (catalytic) radical cascade cyclizations using aryl isocyanides are generally advantageous for installing valuable functional groups in sophisticated patterns within the synthesis.<sup>25,26</sup>

Allenes are also eficient acceptors, with radicals adding predominantly at the central sp-carbon.<sup>28</sup> Thus, we envisioned that the formerly not examined ortho-allenylaryl isocyanides 3 might be eligible substrates for eficient quinoline synthesis, enabling simultaneous C2- and C3-benzylic functionalization (Scheme 1C). While this substrate class 3 was recently studied in Pd-catalyzed living cyclocopolymerizations,<sup>29</sup> to the best of our knowledge no radical transformations have been reported with these interesting acceptors so far. Initial radical addition onto isocyanide 3 would lead to imidoyl radical 4 that should engage in a 6-exo-cyclization to generate benzylic radical 5, which can further react with various trapping reagents. We initiated our studies by examining the 2-allenylaryl isocyanide 3a $\left( \mathbf { R } ^ { \prime } \ = \ \mathbf { H } \right)$ in a radical copper-catalyzed cyanotrifluoromethylation sequence (Scheme 2A).<sup>30</sup> With the Togni II reagent as a CF -radical precursor, $\mathrm { C u } ( \mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C N } ) _ { 4 } \mathrm { P F } _ { 6 }$ as a precatalyst (5.0 mol %), L1 as a ligand (8.5 mol %), and TMSCN (2.4 equiv) as a cyanide source in ethyl acetate, the targeted 3-quinolineacetonitrile 6a was obtained in 73% isolated yield (Scheme 2B, entry 1). Alternative $\cdot _ { \mathrm { C F } _ { 3 } }$ precursors like the Togni I or Umemoto reagents provided 6a in significantly lower yields (Scheme 2B, entries 2 and 3). Performing the Cu-catalyzed cascade reaction without a ligand or with achiral 1,10-phenanthroline led to poor product formation and incomplete conversion of 3a (Scheme 2B, entries 4 and 5). Acetonitrile, a commonly used solvent in related transformations,<sup>31</sup> was found to have a deteriorating efect on the reaction outcome (Scheme 2B, entry 6).

Applying the best conditions, we next examined the scope of this transformation (Scheme 2C). The unsubstituted 6b as well as the halide derivatives 6c and 6d were formed in good yields (55−70%). Notably, using the pyridine-derived isocyanide 3e allowed expansion of the product scope from simple quinolines to naphthyridineacetonitrile 6e (51%). However, for azidation<sup>32,33</sup> or thiocyanatio n,<sup>32,34</sup> applying other nucleophile sources like $\mathrm { T M S N } _ { 3 }$ or TMSNCS provided only traces of the corresponding products 6f and 6g. Togni II derivatives carrying longer perfluoroalkyl chains were eligible radical precursors for the successful preparation of quinolines 6h (62%) and 6i (64%).

Allene acceptors with an additional substituent at the terminal position undergo the addition−cyclization sequence, leading to prochiral secondary benzylic radicals of type 5, eventually afording chiral 3-quinolineacetonitriles 6 through Cu-mediated cyanation. We were very pleased to find that with the methyl-substituted 2-allenylaryl isocyanides 3j−l under our standard conditions with chiral ligand L1 the desired nitriles 6j−l were formed with high enantioselectivity (90−96% ee), albeit in diminished yields (18−51%). This is likely due to competing side reactions as the more nucleophilic disubstituted allenes 3j−l rapidly decompose in solution. Since more electron-rich isocyanides decompose slower,<sup>35</sup> the highest yield in this series was noted for the methoxy derivative 6j (51%). Unfortunately, no further improvement could be achieved in a screening of other chiral bisoxazoline ligands (see SI section 3, Scheme S1 for details). The absolute configuration of 6j was unambiguously assigned by X-ray crystal structure analysis (Xray structure of the R-enantiomer prepared with L1 is depicted in Scheme 2C). The absolute configurations of 6k and 6l were assigned in analogy. Of note, the asymmetric copper-catalyzed cyanation of secondary benzylic radicals has been heavily investigated in recent years.<sup>31,36,37</sup>

In this context, prochiral benzylic radicals for enantioselective cyanation were generated through benzylic hydrogen atom abstraction, fragmentation, or radical addition to styrene derivatives.<sup>37</sup> Thus, our current study discloses a novel synthetic route for the generation of secondary benzylic radicals through concomitant arene construction (Scheme 2D) (for a proposed mechanism, see SI chapter 4, Scheme S2). Atom transfer radical addition (ATRA) cyclization with thiols<sup>14,15</sup> was also examined using our 2-allenylaryl isocyanides 3 as radical acceptors (Scheme 3C). Cyclizing hydrothiolation applying a thermal initiation protocol (method A) with AIBN (α,α′-azoisobutyronitrile) showed a good functional group tolerance as demonstrated by the reaction of 3a with aromatic (9a) and aliphatic (9b−f) thiols. Ester and amide functionalities at the thiol component were tolerated (9f). However, allyl thiol provided 9c in a moderate yield as a result of a thiol−ene reaction on the initially targeted product (see SI for details). Sterically more demanding tertiary thiols aforded the corresponding products 9d and 9e in comparable yields. Noteworthy, natural product-derived scafolds like monoterpene 9e or protected cysteine 9f could be used as the thiol component in these transformations (59−72%). The terminal substituted allene 3j yielded 9h only in traces due to the lower stability of the starting material. Unfortunately, thioacetic acid was not an eligible thiyl radical precursor in this cascade reaction, and 9i was not detected. Along with the thermal protocol, a photochemical initiation process (method B) was successfully applied for the preparation of 9b from allene 3a and butyl thiol (see SI for details).<sup>39</sup> The quinoline 9b was obtained in slightly diminished yields (61%) compared with the thermally initiated process.

In summary, we introduced 2-allenylaryl isocyanides 3 as versatile radical acceptors for the preparation of diversely substituted quinolines 6−9. 2-Trifluoromethylated 3-quinoli neacetonitriles 6 were obtained through copper-catalyzed perfluoroalkylcyanation of 3. With terminal substituted allenes, chiral 3-quinolinacetonitriles 6j−l were accessed with high enantioselectivity (90−96% ee) using a chiral Cu catalyst. Furthermore, ATRA cyclization applying nBu SnH, aromatic, and aliphatic thiols provided various S- and Sn-substituted quinolines. The latter engaged in Stille cross couplings, enabling the formal synthesis of the drug brequinar. While our study examined 2-allenylaryl isocyanides 3 across four diferent radical protocols, each yielding distinct product classes, we anticipate that additional reaction modes could be applicable when isonitriles of type 3 are used as the starting material. Therefore, we encourage others to consider this platform in their synthetic planning.
