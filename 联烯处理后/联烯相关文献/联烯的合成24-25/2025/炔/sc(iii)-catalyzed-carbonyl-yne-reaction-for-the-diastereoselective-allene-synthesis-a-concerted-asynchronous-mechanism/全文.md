Mechanistically, this transformation parallels the classical ene reaction<sup>15,16</sup> but substitutes the “ene” component with a propargylic fragment (Scheme 1a,b). Where classical ene reactions engage an allylic C−H bond, an alkene C�C bond, and an enophile, the carbonyl−yne variant utilizes a propargylic C−H bond, an alkyne C�C bond, and an ynophile (carbonyl group). Investigation of this “propargyl− ene-type” reaction would provide a rational mechanistic explanation and deeper understanding for the stereoselective formation of multisubstituted allene products. Nevertheless, achieving high diastereo- and even enantioselectivity for multisubstituted allenes, particularly sterically congested architectures, remains challenging.<sup>17−20</sup>

Substrates 1v−1z with substituents (Br, F, NO , Me, Ph) on the aniline ring linking the α- ketoamide and alkyne units also performed well, delivering allenes 2v−2z in comparable eficiency (Table 2, entries 22− 26). Finally, alkynes 1aa−1ab featuring benzyl or propargyl groups on the nitrogen atom participated efectively, yielding allenes 2aa (84% yield, 8/1 dr) and 2ab (67% yield, 5.6/1 dr), respectively (Table 2, entries 27−28). However, in the case of alkynes 1ac−1ae bearing secondary alkyl substituents (i-Pr, cyclohexyl, cyclopentyl) at the alkyne terminus, a Sc(III)- catalyzed tandem carbonyl−yne/cycloisomerization reaction occurred, furnishing polycyclic 2,5-dihydrofuran products 2ac−2ae (Table 2, entries 29−30).

To elucidate the origin of diastereoselectivity in the scandium-catalyzed formation of multisubstituted allenes, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed (Figure 1).<sup>24−38</sup> The reaction initiates with coordination of the α-ketoamide group to scandium trifluoromethanesulfonate, forming intermediates INT1 and INT2. These intermediates then undergo C−C bond formation and hydrogen migration, giving INT3 and INT4 via transition states TS1 and TS2, respectively. Subsequent decomplexation yields allenes 2a and 2a′. The calculated Gibbs free energy of activation (ΔG<sup>‡</sup>) for the pathway via TS1 (8.7 kcal/mol) is 1.8 kcal/mol lower than that for the TS2 pathway (10.5 kcal/mol), which is in excellent agreement with the experimentally observed product distribution. Geometric analysis of TS1 indicates a concerted yet asynchronous mechanism.

This asynchronicity is evidenced by the bond lengths within the transition state structure: the forming C1−C2 bond (1.98 Å) is substantially formed, whereas the scissile C3−H bond is marginally elongated to 1.12 from 1.09 Å, and the H−O bond is only 2.16 Å. This disparity demonstrates that C−C bond formation is significantly more advanced than C−H bond cleavage.

A notable finding is that the overall free energy trend is inverted relative to the electronic energies. TS2 is electronically more stable than TS1 by approximately 0.19 kcal/mol. However, the diastereoselectivity is governed by the Gibbs free energy, and the preference for the TS1 pathway is determined by entropic contributions (Table 3). The butyl substituent in TS1 possesses greater conformational and vibrational freedom, which manifests as a larger population of low-frequency vibrational modes. These modes make a substantial contribution to the vibrational entropy, thereby lowering the overall Gibbs free energy of TS1. In contrast, in TS2, the motion of the butyl group is sterically restricted by interactions with the triflate anion. This restriction results in an increase in its vibrational wavenumbers by approximately $5 { - } 1 0 ~ \mathrm { c m ^ { - 1 } }$ relative to TS1, leading to a significant entropic penalty and rendering this pathway less favorable. Furthermore, the influence of the $\mathbb { R } ^ { 1 } \left( \bar { \mathbf { M } } \mathbf { e } , \mathbb { E } \mathrm { t } , i \mathbf { \mathrm { P r } } , t \mathbf { \mathrm { - } } \mathbf { B } \mathbf { u } \right)$ substituent on the ketone group might also be linked to dispersion forces. Interactions between R<sup>1</sup> and the n-butyl on the alkyne group could potentially narrow the electronic energy diference between TS1 and TS2, thereby afecting the reaction’s diastereoselectivity.

To demonstrate the synthetic potential of the Sc-catalyzed carbonyl−yne reaction, we performed a series of transformations on allene 2a (Figure 2). Treatment of 2a with silver(I) triflate induced cyclization, afording dihydrofuran 3 in 76% yield. Alternatively, palladium-catalyzed cyclization/ allylation of 2a produced dihydrofuran 4. Employing Niodosuccinimide (NIS) provided dihydrofuran 5 in 70% yield. Notably, the formation of dihydrofurans 3−5 proceeded diastereoselectively, as confirmed by X-ray crystallographic analysis. Under the promotion of N-bromosuccinimide (NBS), 2a underwent a semipinacol rearrangement featuring 1,2- migration of the isopropyl group. This transformation furnished trisubstituted alkene 6 as the major product, whose structure was unambiguously confirmed by X-ray crystallographic analysis.

In summary, we have developed a Sc(OTf) -catalyzed carbonyl−yne reaction for the diastereoselective synthesis of multisubstituted allenes from α-ketoamide-tethered alkynes. This method features excellent atom economy, a broad substrate scope (tolerating diverse alkyl, aryl, and functional groups), and good-to-excellent diastereoselectivity. Theoretical analysis suggests that the observed stereoselectivity arises from a sophisticated interplay of electronic and entropic efects in the transition states. Meanwhile, this work addresses longstanding challenges in carbonyl−yne chemistry, establishing a robust platform for allene synthesis with applications in targetoriented synthesis. Further investigations into the asymmetric carbonyl−yne reaction and its synthetic applications are underway in our laboratory.
