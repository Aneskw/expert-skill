ABSTRACT: Metal hydride hydrogen atom transfer (MHAT) has emerged as a powerful strategy for alkene hydrofunctionalization, ofering exceptional control over regio- and chemoselectivity. However, conventional MHAT reactions with 1,3-enynes remain limited to the 1,2-addition pathway. Herein, we report a regioselective 1,4-hydroarylation of 1,3-enynes with various aryl halides enabled by dual cobalt and nickel catalysis. This

conversion of simple olefins or alkynes into more complex and diverse compounds, thus facilitating the development of novel materials, pharmaceuticals, and various other valuable products.<sup>1,2</sup> In particular, the first-row transition-metal (e.g., Fe, Mn and Co)-catalyzed metal-hydride hydrogen atom transfer (MHAT) reactions serve as a highly chemoselective means for Markovnikov hydrofunctionalization of alkenes and have witnessed remarkable progress.<sup>3</sup> Intermediate carbon-centered radicals derived from MHAT exhibit versatile reactivity, with recent advances highlighting their synthetic potential and mechanistic versatility, including participation in classical radical transformations (e.g., atom/group transfer and addition to unsaturated bonds)<sup>4,5</sup> as well as subsequent oxidation or reduction to However, in contrast to the well-developed hydrofunctional ization of simple styrene and unactivated alkenes, MHATinitiated hydrofunctionalization of 1,3-enynes�versatile synthons featuring both alkenyl and alkynyl moieties�remains elusive and underexplored.<sup>9</sup> Such dificulty is unsurprising, given the inherent challenges associated with the regiochemical complexity, multiple possibilities, and potential overreactions that exist as competing pathways that hinder the achievement of high regioselectivity.<sup>10</sup> Seminal work by Carreira demonstrated that cobalt(III)-hydride-initiated MHAT enables selective hydrohydrazination of enynes, in which the propargyl radical A generated in situ was directly addition to azodicarboxylates, delivering a series of propargylic hydrazines in useful yields (Scheme 1b).<sup>9</sup> Despite recently there have been relevant attempts to test the feasibility of enyne in MHAT reactions, successful functionalization has thus far been confined to the 1,2-addition paradigm with alkynyl retention.<sup>7c</sup> In contrast, direct 1,4-hydrofunctionalization�a highly desirable route to synthetically valuable allene derivatives<sup>11</sup> remains underexplored.

Recent works including our own have unveiled MHATmediated hydrofunctionalizations of 1,3-dienes, in which a regioselective 1,2- or 1,4-hydrofunctionalization can be achieved through selective hydrogen-atom transfer from the cobalt hydride species.<sup>12</sup> Inspired by these studies and due to our continuous interest in Co(III)H-catalyzed radical reactions,<sup>8,12d</sup> we questioned whether a propargylic radical could be merged with Co(III)-H catalysis to enable 1,4-hydroarylation of enynes. We envisioned that MHAT from Co(III)- H would initiate formation of propargyl radical/cobalt(II) pairs, followed by isomerization to generate an allenylcobalt-(III) intermediate C (Scheme 1a right), which can be intercepted by catalytic organometal species such as catalytic organonickel species, that would enable direct 1,4-hydrofunctionalization of 1,3-enynes to yield multisubstituted allenes (Scheme 1b bottom). Successfully accomplishing this process would expand the utilization of organocobalt to analogous allenyl-Co(III) intermediates, significantly less explored in cobalt-catalyzed MHAT transformation. Notably, the Shenvi group creatively developed a Ni/Co(II)-catalyzed reductive coupling hydroarylation of unactivated terminal alkenes through an unprecedented radical alkyl-Co(III) to nickel direct transmetalation.<sup>6a,13</sup> Herein, we reported the first investigation of cobalt-hydride HAT initiated radical 1,4-hydroarylation of 1,3-enynes with aryl halides via dual Co/Ni catalysis (Scheme 1c). This reaction provides an eficient and straightforward approach to synthesize valuable allenes featuring four diferent functional group moieties in good to excellent yields.

We commenced this study by choosing 1-methoxy-4-(non-2-en-4-yn-3-yl)benzene (1a) and p-trifluoromethyl iodobenzene (2a) as substrates. Pleasingly, under optimized conditions using Co<sup>III</sup>(Salen) complex [Co(III)-1] as catalyst, NiBr · glyme and 4-methoxyl-substituted bpy (L1) as cocatalyst, and PhSiH (O<sup>i</sup>Pr) as the hydride source in DMPU at 40 ${ } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the desired allene 3aa was obtained in 93% yield (entry 1, Table 1). Control experiments established the critical roles of each reaction component (entries 2−4). Notably, no product was observed in the absence of silanes, demonstrating their essential function in facilitating Co(III)-mediated MHAT reactions and potentially generating catalytically active Ni−H species (entry 4). Based on the previous works reported by Shenvi,<sup>6a</sup> we employed NFTPB (4) as an extra oxidant in combination with catalytic Co(II)Salen complex, which efectively facilitates the conversion of Co(II) to Co(III) and sustains the cobalt catalytic $\mathrm { c y c l e } , ^ { 7 }$ afording the desired product 3aa in 65% yield (entry 5). In comparison, the direct use of the Co(III)Salen complex is more efective and can avoid the requirement for stoichiometric oxidants. Other silanes, such as TMDSO and $\mathrm { P h } S \mathrm { i H } _ { 3 } ,$ were also efective in facilitating the reaction, albeit with decreased yields (entries $^ { 6 , }$ 7). Switching the Co(III)-1 to its analogue Co(III)-3 or

Co(III)-4 also gave allene 3aa in 35−42% yield, but the utilization of Co(III)-2 failed to deliver 3aa (entries 8, 9). The use of other nickel catalysts and ligands in place of NiBr · glyme/L1 gave acceptable results (entries 10−13). When employing other solvents, such as CH CN and DCE, all failed to deliver the desired product (entry 14).

With the optimized reaction conditions in hand, we next investigated the scope of allene synthesis with respect to the substituents (R<sup>1</sup>−R<sup>3</sup>) on enynes (Scheme 2). As for the substituent R<sup>1</sup> on the aryl moiety, the method was suitable for installing various electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents, including methoxy, alkyl, fluoro, chloro, and trifluoromethyl, all of which were conveniently transformed to the expected tetrasubstituted allene products 3bb−3kb in good to excellent yields. The process was not detrimentally influenced by the position (o-, m-, p-) of the substituent on the phenyl ring; even ortho-substitution, which was regarded as having a certain steric hindrance, can deliver the expected products with outstanding yields. Similarly, the reaction of disubstituted aryl (for 3lb−3nb) as well as electron-rich arene (for 3ob) and naphthalene (for 3pa) substituted 1,3-enynes also proceeded smoothly to aford allenes in good yields. In addition, the present transformation was not disturbed by the substituent $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ tethered to the alkene moiety. It was shown that a variety of enynes having an internal substituent $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ on the alkene motif, providing desired products 3qb−3tb in 73−78% yields. Besides the R<sup>2</sup>-substituted internal alkenes, employing a terminal alkene moiety also performed well (see 3ub−3xb). As for $\mathbb { R } ^ { 3 }$ tethered on the alkynyl moiety, alkyl groups with diferent lengths (for 3Ab−3Db), functionalized primary alkyl groups (for 3Eb and 3Fb), and sterically bulkier cycloalkyl groups (for 3Gc) and cyclohexenyl (3Hb) as well as ether (3Ib) groups could be installed, delivering the corresponding tetrasubstituted allenes in moderate to good yields. The tertbutyl-substituted 1,3-enyne failed to yield 3Jb, likely due to excessive steric hindrance.

The reaction scope was then extended to various aryl electrophilic reagents (Scheme 3). A wide array of aryl iodides, including those with electron-donating or electron-withdrawing groups, underwent smooth coupling with 1,3-enyne to deliver allene products 4a−4i in good to excellent yields. Substrates with multiple fluorine substituents (4m, 4n), heteroaryl iodides (4o, 4p), and diverse meta- or disubstituted arenes including electron-deficient 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl)- iodobenzene (4j−4l, 4s−4w) all reacted smoothly to deliver products in high yields. For broader synthetic interests, 4-CNand 4-COOMe-substituted iodobenzenes were also viable to deliver the corresponding allene products 4q and 4r with yields of 91% and 87%, respectively. In contrast, an alkyl iodide failed to couple and led to only partial oxidation of 1,3-enyne. Substrate evaluation beyond aryl iodides revealed that bromo and chloro arenes were also viable coupling partners, afording 3aa in 65% and 37% yield, respectively, whereas aryl triflates failed to react. Furthermore, the synthetic utility was demonstrated on a 3.0 mmol scale, delivering 3aa in 80% yield. Moreover, some bioactive molecules, such as cholesterol, D-(−)-pantolactone, and structurally more complicated vitamin E-derivated aryl iodides were conveniently transformed to the corresponding allene products 4x−4z in good yields, which demonstrated that this method would be suitable for late-stage functionalization of complicated molecules.

A series of experiments were performed to probe the reaction mechanism (Scheme 4). First, the introduction of a stoichiometric amount of $^ { 2 , 2 , 6 , 6 }$ -tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) significantly attenuated the transformation, while no TEMPO-trapped product 6 was detected (Scheme 4a). In contrast, a “radical-clock” experiment with (2- vinylcyclopropyl)benzene 7 led to ring-opened product 8 in 38% yield (Scheme 4b). In addition, diene 9 underwent cyclization to give product 10 with 35% yield and 5:1 dr (Scheme 4b). These phenomena reveal a radical mechanism, which was further corroborated by the detection of a minor coupling byproduct derived from propargyl radical and allenyl radicals (see SI for details). A deuterium labeling experiment using $\mathrm { P h } _ { 2 } \mathrm { S i D } _ { 2 } \left( > 9 5 \% \mathrm { D } \right)$ aforded $d _ { \mathrm { 1 } } ^ { \mathrm { ~ ~ } }$ -3cb with 0.95 D exclusive incorporation at the alkene terminus, confirming the silane as the hydrogen source and supporting a CoH-mediated MHAT pathway (Scheme 4c).

To identify the active organometallic species in this crosscoupling process, the isolable nickel(II) oxidative addition intermediate 11 was synthesized (Scheme 4d) and evaluated in control experiments.<sup>14</sup> When complex 11 was employed as the nickel precatalyst in the reaction of 1,3-enyne (1c) with ethyl 4-iodobenzoate (2u) under standard conditions, allene 12 was obtained in 58% yield (Scheme 4e, eq 1). In contrast, a stoichiometric reaction of 11 with 1c in the absence of aryl iodide aforded only trace amounts of 12 (<5% yield, eq 2). Interestingly, when an additional 1.5 equiv of 4-chloroiodobenzene (2b) was employed as the coupling reagent, it was found that the 4-chloroaryl-substituted allene 3cb was obtained in 54% yield, but still with only 5% of product 12 originated from the “precatalyst” 11 (Scheme 4e, eq 3). The low yield of product 12 seems insuficient to support an arylnickel(II) species as a feasible intermediate in the catalytic cycle, and the mechanism that directs trapping of the propargyl/allenyl radical derived from MHAT by an arylnickel(II) species to form Ni(III) species followed by reductive elimination to form coupled products can be excluded (the aryl iodide was not consumed).<sup>15</sup> Meanwhile, in the absence of an oxidative agent, the continuous production of Co−H is also halted. Theoretically, at most, 10% of the C−C coupling products will be formed. These results further encouraged us to consider that Ni(II) complex 11 might transform into an active Ni catalyst (e.g., Ni(I)) to prompt the reaction to occur.

## ■ CONCLUSION

In summary, we have developed an eficient MHAT-mediated 1,4-hydroarylation of 1,3-enynes with aryl halides via synergistic cobalt/nickel catalysis. This MHAT-mediated protocol provides direct access to diverse tetrasubstituted allenes in high yields. These allene products serve as versatile synthetic platforms for further elaboration through alkene functionalization. This work expands the arsenal of MHAT reactions by merging Co−H-mediated MHAT with Nicatalyzed cross-coupling, ofering a novel strategy for allene synthesis. Studies on the mechanism and development of enantioselective variants are currently underway.
