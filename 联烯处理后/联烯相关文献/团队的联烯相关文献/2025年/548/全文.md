# Photoredox copper-catalyzed regioselective syntheses of allenols via HAT-mediated propargyl radical generation†

Wenyao Li,<sup>a</sup> Hao Yu \*<sup>a</sup> and Shengming Ma \*<sup>a,b</sup>

Received 8th July 2024,

Accepted 7th September 2024

DOI: 10.1039/d4qo01245c

rsc.li/frontiers-organic

Selectivity control of the reactions of propargylic and/or allenylic radicals remains particularly elusive. Here, a visible light-mediated Cu-catalyzed protocol for the highly selective syntheses of tri- and tetrasubstituted allenols from alkynol derivatives has been developed. The synthetic potential of the products has been demonstrated. The control experiments, Stern–Volmer quenching, and cyclic voltammetry studies support a reaction pathway involving the generation of allenyl radicals <sub>v</sub>i<sub>a</sub> photo-mediated N–O bond scission, an intramolecular 1,5-HAT process, the resonance to propargylic radicals, and coupling. A non-common intramolecular 1,6-HAT process has also been observed.

## Introduction

Selectivity control is an everlasting topic in organic synthesis, advanced materials, and medicinal chemistry or life science. For example, it is of particular interest to see the chemistry on the propargylic/allenylic species due to the versatile potential of alkynes and allenes. Recently, much attention has been paid to the generation and reactivity of propargylic and/or allenylic radicals afording alkynes<sup>2</sup> and/or allenes.<sup>3–6</sup> However, the eficient generation of propargylic radicals and the selectivity-controlled trapping still remain particularly elusive (Fig. 1a). 1,5-Hydrogen atom transfer (HAT)<sup>7,8</sup> mediated by alkoxyl radicals<sup>9,10</sup> has emerged as an important strategy for $\mathrm { C } ( \mathrm { s p } ^ { 3 } ) \mathrm { - H }$ bond functionalization (Fig. 1b). Given the high eficiency of radical formation with this specific method, we wonder whether such an alkoxyl radical-induced HAT of alkynes would eficiently generate propargylic radicals followed by transition metal-catalyzed functionalization, providing an eficient and selective platform for synthesizing multi-substituted alkynes or allenes (Fig. 1c). However, notable challenges for such a concept included (Fig. 1c): firstly, the tautomeric equilibrium of propargylic radicals to allenyl radicals and the resulting control of selectivity for trapping of these two radicals;<sup>2–4</sup> secondly, the introduction of the alkyne functional group and the formation of the allene functional group may lead to direct cyclic radical addition reactions;<sup>11</sup> and thirdly, the facile HAT of allenyl radicals could produce other nonfunctionalized allene by-products before the transition metalcatalyzed functionalization occurs. In addition, it would be a platform reaction for the study on how far a HAT reaction may go. Here, we report our success on the exclusive synthesis of tri- and tetra-substituted allenes via visible-light photoredox and copper dual catalysis (Fig. 1d).

## Results and discussion

Our initial attempt used N-alkynoxyphthalimide 1a, which is easily obtained by the Mitsunobu reaction from the corresponding alkynol. We began investigating the coupling reaction of 1a with trimethylsilyl cyanide by applying a catalytic system consisting of 4CzIPN and CuCl/dmbpy in $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C N }$ under blue LED irradiation, and the desired allene product $\mathbf { 2 } \mathbf { a } ^ { \prime }$ was aforded in 38% yield as determined by <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectroscopy (Table 1, entry 1), without the formation of an alkyne product. The photocatalyst $\mathrm { { \bf M e s A c r ^ { + } C l O _ { 4 } } ^ { - } }$ resulted in a largely decreased yield of the allene product 2a′ (Table 1, entry 2). Further exploration of photocatalysts revealed that iridium complexes, such as Ir[(dtbbpy)( ppy)<sub>2</sub>]PF<sub>6</sub> or Ir(ppy)<sub>3</sub>, were more eficient (Table 1, entries 3 and 4). Other copper catalysts such as CuBr and CuI (Table 1, entries 5 and 6) were also screened and CuCl was still the best. Subsequently, we tested a series of other bipyridine ligands, and identified 1,10-phenanthroline as equally excellent as dmbpy (Table 1, entry 7). We chose 1,10-phen as the ligand of preference finally in consideration of the price. Other alkynoxylamide derivatives, such as 3a and 4a, exhibited a much lower reactivity (Table 1, entries 8 and 9). Control experiments demonstrated that the CuCl/1,10- phen system, photocatalyst and light are indispensable for enabling this transformation to proceed smoothly (entries 10–12).

With the optimized conditions in hand, we conducted further investigations on the substrate scope of this reaction (Fig. 2). We found that a wide range of terminal alkynols are suitable for this transformation, leading to the successful preparation of the corresponding tri-substituted allenols 2a–2t smoothly. In terms of the alkyl groups $\mathbf { R } ^ { 1 }$ at the propargyl position, non-functionalized linear primary alkyl chains of various lengths (2a–2d) as well as alkyl substituents bearing ethers (2e and 2f), chloro (2g), acetal (2h), terminal olefin (2i), and aryls (2j–2m) were all found to be compatible under the standard conditions. $\mathbf { R } ^ { 1 }$ may also be secondary alkyl groups (2n–2p) or even a tertiary alkyl group (2q), resulting in the corresponding allenols in modest to good yields. Furthermore, beyond the alkyl-substituted substrates, the reaction of the substrate with $\mathbf { R } ^ { 1 }$ as phenyl yielded 2r smoothly, achieving a yield of 66%. Furthermore, the synthesis of secondary allenols 2s and 2t was realized with yields of 76% and 60%, respectively. Finally, this protocol also worked for internal alkynols with $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ being alkyl, phenylalkyl or phenyl groups, afording tetra-substituted allenols (2u–2ae).

Furthermore, various N-alkynoxyphthalimide precursors with diverse tether sizes were employed to investigate the potential of our protocol for 1,4- or 1,6-HAT (Fig. 3). When subjected to the standard conditions, compound 1af failed to yield the corresponding beta-allenol 2af, with the complete consumption of the starting material observed (Fig. 3a). Conversely, the reaction of substrate 1ag with TMSCN resulted in the formation of delta-allenol 2ag in 45% yield, involving 1,6-HAT. Furthermore, endeavors to synthesize the initial N-alkynoxyphthalimide precursor for alpha-allenols proved unsuccessful (see Fig. S4 in the ESI†).

To demonstrate the synthetic potential of this reaction, a gram-scale reaction was conducted using 7 mmol of 1a, resulting in the formation of allenol 2a with an isolated yield of 73% (1.1 g) (Fig. 4a). To gain mechanistic insights into the reaction, firstly, we conducted the reaction of 1a in the presence of radical trapping agents such as TEMPO and BHT. As shown in Fig. 5a, the yield of the desired allenols dropped dramatically with the increase in the amount of the radical inhibitor. In addition, when BHT was used, the alkynol product S1a, resulting from intermolecular HAT of the initially formed alkoxyl radical, was detected. When the internal alkyne 1ah substituted with the triisopropylsilyl group was subjected to the standard conditions, in addition to the normal allene product 2a, the isolation of the tetrahydrofuranyl triisopropylsilyl acetylene product 13ah indicated the involvement of a propargylic cation, which may be formed via the oxidation of a propargylic radical intermediate (Fig. 5b). Moreover, Stern–Volmer quenching experiments (Fig. 5c) demonstrated that the CuCl/ 1,10-phen catalyst, rather than the substrate 1a, quenched the excited-state photocatalyst $\operatorname { I r } ( { \mathsf { p p y } } ) _ { 3 } ,$ suggesting a reductive quenching pathway. ## Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a unified platform for the highly selective preparation of tri- and tetra-substituted allenols. This approach leverages dual copper and photoredox catalysis to enable the generation and functionalization of allenyl radicals. This mild reaction demonstrates a broad substrate scope, and the post-reaction transformations of the allenol products highlight the significant synthetic potential of this methodology.
